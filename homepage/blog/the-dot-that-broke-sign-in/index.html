Behind the scenes

The Dot That Broke Sign-In

September 7, 2026

A reader told us they couldn't log in. Not "forgot my password" — actually couldn't, no matter what they typed. Chasing that down turned into one of those bugs that starts small and keeps getting bigger the more you look at it, and ended with us fixing something that had probably been quietly breaking sign-in for most real email addresses since the day self-service registration launched.

The report

It started simply: someone registered, clicked the confirmation link in their email, and got a plain "File not found." That part was easy — the link we were sending pointed at the wrong part of our own site, a one-line fix. We shipped it, said so, and figured that was that.

It wasn't. The same reader tried again and got a new error: Invalid credentials. Wrong username or password, as far as our own login screen was concerned. Except they were copying the exact username and password we'd just shown them.

Chasing it down

Planagonia's accounts live in WordPress behind the scenes — not something you ever see, just the system that actually checks a password is correct. When we looked at exactly what our own code was sending it, and exactly what WordPress was sending back, we found something we hadn't expected: WordPress hands back a person's account information as two different names. One is the real login — the thing you'd actually type in to sign in. The other is a cleaned-up, web-address-safe version of the same name, used for things like a public profile link — and it turns a dot into a dash.

Our own code had been reading the wrong one of those two names the whole time, and showing that to people as if it were their real username.

For a username like janedoe, both versions happen to be identical — dashes and dots only differ when there's a dot to begin with. So this had been invisible for exactly as long as nobody had tested it with an email address that actually contains one. Most real email addresses look like first.last@example.com. We'd been quietly showing a huge share of people a username that could never, ever have worked.

The fix, and the safety net we built alongside it

The immediate fix was straightforward once we understood it: read the right name, everywhere we'd been reading the wrong one. We also made sign-in accept an email address directly, not just a username — turns out the underlying check already supported it, we just weren't telling anyone.

But the report also pointed at something bigger: if you ever lost the one-time password we show you right after confirming your email, there was genuinely no way to get another one. Not a small oversight — a real dead end. So we built one: a proper "Forgot your password?" link that emails you a fresh sign-in credential, reusing the exact same confirmation flow that already worked correctly for a first-time signup. It works whether you lost your original password, or you're someone whose account existed before self-service registration was even a feature.

Signed up a while ago and never quite got sign-in working? It's fixed now — and if you ever get stuck again, open the app and look for "Forgot your password?" right under the sign-in form.

What this one taught us

The satisfying kind of bug hunt is the one where each answer raises exactly one more question, until you're standing somewhere you didn't expect to be. "File not found" led to "invalid credentials" led to a mixed-up field name led to "there's no way to recover from this at all" led to a whole feature we hadn't planned on building that day. None of it would have surfaced from us testing our own account — our own usernames don't have dots in them either. It took someone else, hitting it for real, to show us.
